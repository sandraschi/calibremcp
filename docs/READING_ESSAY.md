# How Reading Went Electric — a meta essay

*Why your library lives on a disk now, what that did to readers, writers, and
money — and where it goes next. Long read; pour something warm.*

---

## 1. In the beginning was the mainframe

On 4 July 1971, Michael Hart — a student with access to a Xerox Sigma V
mainframe at the University of Illinois — typed the US Declaration of
Independence into a computer and gave the file away. That file became
[Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org), and Hart spent the rest of his
life arguing a beautifully stubborn thesis: anything in the public domain
belongs to everyone, in the cheapest possible format, forever.

For thirty years almost nobody listened. Ebooks existed, technically, the way a
seed exists in winter. The soil — a device you would actually read on, a store
that sold files instead of objects, a format publishers accepted — arrived
decades later. But the seed mattered: when the industry finally moved, the
free public-domain corpus was already sitting there, waiting, 70,000 books
deep. Every "free classics" section of every ebook store is downstream of one
student's fireworks-night typing session.

## 2. The wilderness years (1990–2007)

The nineties and early two-thousands were a parade of almosts:

- **Palm Pilots and Peanut Press** (later eReader): thousands of people read
  entire novels on 160-pixel greenish screens with a stylus. Voluntarily. The
  desire was already there; the display wasn't.
- **Rocket eBook and SoftBook (1998)**: the first dedicated readers. Heavy,
  expensive, tethered to desktop software, stores that barely existed. Both
  dead within a few years.
- **Sony Librie (2004)** and the PRS-500 (2006): the first E Ink readers — and
  Sony's proprietary BBeB format, which is exactly why a young Kovid Goyal
  started reverse-engineering it on Linux in October 2006. That side project
  became [Calibre](https://calibre-ebook.com). (See Help → About Calibre.)

The lesson of the wilderness: the reader was never the hard part. The hard
parts were the *store*, the *format*, and the * publishers' permission*.

## 3. The Kindle detonation (2007)

Amazon launched the Kindle in November 2007 at $399 — and sold out in hours.
Three decisions made it stick where everything else bounced:

1. **Whispernet**: free cellular connectivity. Buying a book took sixty seconds
   with no computer, no cables, no syncing. Friction went from an afternoon to
   a minute.
2. **A store, not a device**: Amazon already had your credit card and your
   trust. The Kindle was a vending machine for a shop you lived in.
3. **E Ink at scale**: finally cheap and good enough for beach reading.

Publishers, terrified, answered with the **agency model** (2010, Apple as
co-conspirator): publishers set prices, stores take 30%. The US Department of
Justice sued, Apple lost in 2013, and ebooks got cheaper for a while — then
quietly expensive again. The commercial shape of ebooks today (roughly $10–15
for a new release, wild west for backlist and indie) is the scar tissue of
that fight.

## 4. Formats and the DRM saga

The format war ended the way these wars do: not with victory, with exhaustion.
EPUB (open standard, maintained by the W3C these days) won everywhere except
inside Amazon's walls, where MOBI gave way to AZW3/KF8 and then KFX. Calibre
converts between nearly all of them, which is why it survived every round.

DRM deserves its own paragraph because it shaped reader psychology more than
any format. Adobe's ADEPT and Amazon's Kindle DRM mean, strictly speaking, you
rent the *ability* to read with permission attached: device limits, no lending
(except the anaemic official kind), books that can be edited or withdrawn
remotely. (Yes, the irony of *1984* vanishing from Kindles in 2009 is noted,
and will never stop being cited.) The market response was rational: readers who
learned this migrated toward DRM-free sellers — O'Reilly, No Starch, Tor,
Smashwords — and toward Calibre as the place where *their* files live
regardless of which store sold them. Your library folder is a small act of
independence. Treat it that way: keep originals, keep receipts.

## 5. E-ink versus glass

Two philosophies of screen, both winning different rooms:

- **E Ink** (Kindle, Kobo, PocketBook, reMarkable): reflective, weeks of
  battery, sunlight-readable, no flicker. The closest thing to paper physics.
  Refresh rates and color (Kaleido, Gallery) keep improving; note-taking
  tablets made "reader" and "notebook" overlap.
- **Glass** (phones, tablets): backlit, fast, color-perfect — and full of
  notifications. The best-selling "e-reader" in the world is probably the
  iPhone Kindle app.

Nobody won; the market segmented by *context*. E Ink owns the couch and the
beach. Glass owns the commute, the midnight chapter, and comics in full color.

## 6. What screens do to readers (the psychology bit)

This is the chapter people argue about at dinner, so let's be precise. The
research literature — Mangen et al. on screen vs. paper comprehension (2013),
Kindle vs. pocket book plot reconstruction (2019), and the Clinton/Delgado
meta-analyses of dozens of studies — converges on roughly this:

- **Small but robust print advantage for long-form comprehension**, especially
  for reconstructing *where* things happened in a text. The leading explanation
  is spatial: fixed pages give memory a landscape; infinite scroll gives it a
  treadmill.
- **Worse calibration on screens**: readers *think* they understood equally
  well. Overconfidence is the quiet tax.
- **The effect shrinks** with familiar devices, short texts, and purpose-driven
  reading (searching, skimming — screens win those outright).
- **Accessibility reverses the story**: adjustable type, line spacing,
  dyslexia-friendly fonts, text-to-speech, and dictionary lookup make ebooks
  strictly better reading for millions of people. No contest.

Translation for your library: literary fiction and long argument → consider
paper or E Ink, slowly. Reference, skimming, travel, midnight, tired eyes →
the screen is not the enemy, it's the tool. Anyone selling you guilt or
triumphalism about either is selling something.

## 7. Who gets paid (the commerce bit)

Follow the money and the last twenty years make sense:

- **Self-publishing** (Kindle Direct Publishing, 2007): royalties jumped from
  ~10% to up to 70%, gatekeepers optional. Romance, SFF, and nonfiction
  niches moved first and never looked back.
- **Fanfiction → trad**: Archive of Our Own (2007, Hugo-winning 2019) proved
  the audience; Fifty Shades and its descendants proved the pipeline.
- **BookTok** (2020–): short video selling backlist by vibes resurrected
  midlist authors and broke print supply chains. Publishers now market like
  influencers because influencers outsell them.
- **Libraries**: OverDrive/Libby made "borrow the ebook" normal; publishers
  answered with metered licenses (a library "copy" that expires after N
  lends). The fight over lending terms is the agency-model war, round two.
- **Germany's special case**: the *Buchpreisbindung* (fixed book price law)
  covers ebooks too — no Amazon-style race to the bottom, which is why Thalia
  and Hugendubel are still standing and your local bookshop still exists.
  But every shield has an edge: fixed prices mean a student, a nurse, anyone
  neither rich nor stupid, faces the same €24.99 for a new ebook as everyone
  else — and learns within minutes that Anna has it for free. The law meant to
  protect German publishers quietly trains price-sensitive readers to leave the
  legal market entirely. A footgun, painted as consumer protection.

## 8. Reading in public went private (the culture bit)

A paperback advertises; an ereader keeps secrets. The train carriage used to
be a census of taste — now it's a row of identical black rectangles. We lost
the cover glance and gained the freedom to read anything anywhere: erotica at
breakfast, doorstop fantasy on a crowded U6, the guilty pleasure with no
witnesses. Romance revenues suggest this privacy had a direct commercial
effect. Nobody can prove it and everybody knows it.

Meanwhile reading got *quantified* (Goodreads streaks, StoryGraph stats,
reading journals) and *social* in new shapes (readathons, buddy reads,
annotated screenshots). The book club didn't die; it got a Discord.

## 9. The quiet superpowers (QoL, the reason we stay)

Strip away the ideology and ebooks win on eleven small conveniences no paper
book will ever match: dictionary lookup, full-text search, adjustable type,
night mode, one-hand reading, weight (a library weighs 200 grams), instant
delivery, sync across devices, text-to-speech, X-ray-style character maps, and
never losing your place. Each is minor. Together they are why even paper
loyalists keep a reader in their bag. Our webapp leans into exactly these:
search-inside, semantic "books about X", reading queues, one-click reader.

## 10. Two readers, one file

An ebook means opposite things at opposite ends of money. Picture a poor
student in Bangalore: one secondhand Android phone, metered data, no bookshop
within reach stocking what she needs. For her the ebook is *access itself* —
a full university library compressed into a device she already owns, priced at
zero via Gutenberg, IA, or the grey channels. Format snobbery is a luxury she
cannot afford and does not need; a badly OCR'd PDF that teaches her Python
beats the printed edition she will never touch.

Picture the opposite: a literature professor with floor-to-ceiling shelves, ten
out of ten on the bibliophile scale. He will keep buying dead-wood books
forever — signed firsts, fine press, the smell — and no argument about
convenience will move him a millimeter, because for him the book was never
primarily a delivery vehicle for text. It is furniture, memory palace, tribe
badge, retirement fund.

Both are rational. The file serves the student; the object serves the
professor. The industry's eternal mistake is designing for one of them while
lecturing the other. Calibre, bless it, serves both: the same tool that
dedupes 10,000 grey PDFs also catalogs a first-edition shelf (photograph the
spines, OCR them into inventory — the poor man's rare-book catalog).

## 11. The beautiful object (bibliophilia, or reading as a rich man's hobby)

Some books are not for reading. A Gutenberg bible leaf, a Kelmscott Chaucer, a
first-edition *Scarlet Pimpernel* with the dust jacket intact — these are
sculptures that happen to contain text, and their market behaves like art, not
publishing: scarcity, provenance, condition, auction drama. First-edition
collecting is a rich person's hobby the way yacht racing is a sport —
technically open to all, practically gated by money.

This is worth saying plainly because it defuses the paper-vs-screen holy war:
the people who will never give up paper *were never arguing about reading*.
They are arguing about objects, and objects are lovely. Let them have it.
Meanwhile the illuminated manuscript — the actual pinnacle of the book as
object, vellum and gold leaf — survives today mostly as *pixels*: the Vatican,
the BnF's Gallica, the British Library, and Switzerland's e-codices have put
thousands of them on the web in higher fidelity than any museum vitrine
allows. The most beautiful books ever made are now ebooks. Nobody planned
that irony; enjoy it.

## 12. Where this is going

Three currents, no predictions beyond them:

1. **AI narration** is crossing the uncanny line — your backlog may soon read
   itself to you in a decent voice.
2. **Your library becomes queryable** — RAG over your own shelves (which this
   app already does) turns a collection into a conversation partner. The
   pioneers of this were the same instinct as Hart's: the text wants to be
   *used*, not stored.
3. **The rights fight isn't over** — the training-data cases (see Help →
   Copyright & AI) will decide what "buying a book" funds in the 2030s. Keep
   receipts, prefer DRM-free, and pay the authors you love directly when you can.
